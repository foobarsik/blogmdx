Scope: this focuses on the US and European markets, including remote work from those regions, and on the first 5–10 years of a career. Other regions need different strategies. So does selling a unique, niche expertise — at that point it's sales, not a job search: you approach a chosen segment as a seller rather than replying to postings. Working for the US from Asia is harder still: the time-zone gap and employer preferences work against you, so talk to local specialists for that case.

### The main principle: search through people

Mass-applying through job boards, company sites, or auto-apply bots converts poorly. It can supplement a search, but it should not be the main channel. Live people — referrals and direct contact — convert far better.

Use the through-people approach when:

- **You need work fast** — contacts are quicker than the queue.
- **You want the right role**, not the first one — especially early-career or niche.
- **You're improving your current position** — mass-applying is acceptable only when you already have a job and are testing the market or practicing interviews. It's slow.

| Your situation | Best channel | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Need work fast | Through people | Contacts skip the queue |
| Want the right role, not the first | Through people | Especially early-career or niche |
| Improving from a stable job | Mass-applying is OK | Only to test the market or practice interviews — it's slow |

Why mass-applying fails:

- The candidate pool is large and uneven; AI-generated resumes have made it worse.
- Companies filter with ATS parsers, spam filters, and outsourced screeners. These make mistakes — a strong candidate can be filtered out roughly half the time, and a recruiter may never see the resume at all.

### When to look

Judging the market from social media, layoffs, or expert takes gives a distorted picture. People who struggle are visible; people who found a job in two days through a contact stay quiet. The market is cyclical and seasonal:

- Companies have different fiscal years (starting in January, June, etc.), and hiring budgets track them.
- Some countries have vacation seasons (e.g. Germany) when activity drops.
- In large tech hubs there are always openings, including unpublished ones you hear about first through meetups and contacts.

The strongest position is to look while employed. Searching without a job is weak leverage: if a recruiter sees urgency, they can push the salary down. Expect a long search, be ready to decline unsuitable offers.

### Resume

A resume exists to pass ATS and give a recruiter a reason to talk — not to be a work of art. Rewriting it obsessively for every posting doesn't fix low conversion if you're applying where you aren't expected.

A good resume is minimal and tells one coherent story: who you are, what you specialize in, and experience that backs it up.

- **Write achievements with numbers and context** — the metric, and how it was measured. Instead of "wrote code and shipped features": "Shipped a feature that raised sales ~10% by improving UX, measured via analytics."
- **List technologies in the context of each project or company**, not as a separate wall of tags.
- **Use a plain ATS-compatible template** (e.g. Google Docs → PDF). Simple structure parses correctly ~80% of the time; reordering blocks (company / title / dates) breaks some parsers.
- **The headline should say who you are** (senior, mid, manager), and the experience below should confirm it.
- **If you have two distinct profiles** (e.g. IC and management), keep two resumes and mention the secondary experience in a single line rather than merging both.
- **Cut irrelevant detail** (talks, hobbies) unless it maps to the role — one line at most.

Over-optimization is a failure mode: stacking too many achievements or profiles (developer + mentor + manager) makes it unclear what role you want. Focus on one and back it with experience.

Keep it minimal for another reason: any detail that enables discrimination (age, gender, photo, date of birth, a hard-to-pronounce name) can be used against you, and no one audits how resumes are filtered or states the reason for rejection. US resumes are deliberately anonymized; Europe is similar despite anti-discrimination law.

A resume is read late — after screenings, by a hiring manager or interviewer who may just check LinkedIn instead. Make it skimmable: bold the things that matter (numbers, achievements) and keep a plain structure. Icons, non-standard fonts, or skill progress bars ("4 of 5 stars in JavaScript") read as amateur and push a resume onto the reject pile.

The ATS is the main obstacle: an automatic parser that breaks a resume into tags. Some ignore an entire experience block if company, title, and dates are in the wrong order, so fill a standard template as-is. One plain template covers ~80% of the market — Greenhouse and other serious systems parse it well; smaller ones are worse. At FAANG scale (50–100 filtered candidates a day per role) the gap between 80% and 100% parsing matters; at a small company it doesn't. ATS-simulator sites that score your resume and sell fixes aren't worth it, and A/B-testing resume variants is ineffective.

A resume also outlives the application. ATS and third-party candidate databases store resumes, work history, and recruiter notes — and they get sold, so an old resume can resurface years later.

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn matters more than the resume. Recruiters search there and cross-check it against your resume, so treat it as the master version — more detail, more results.

- **Set the location correctly.** If you're in one country but targeting another, list the target location or you won't appear in local searches.
- **State relocation up front:** "Open to relocation, need visa sponsorship for Germany / UK / US" in the first lines. Most recruiters send cold, automated outreach without reading; a target location gets you into more searches.
- **Describe achievements in more detail than the resume**, with results and numbers.
- **Claim more, not less.** Undersell is common; if it feels like bragging, it's probably still modest.
- **Feedback is slow** (~6 months). The quality of inbound recruiter messages tells you whether the profile works.

### Location, relocation, and remote

Location drives hiring through taxes and jurisdiction, not just time zones.

- **Keep the resume location consistent with LinkedIn.** State it in the first line: "Based in [X], seeking relocation to [Y], need visa sponsorship." Raise it with the recruiter first thing to confirm fit.
- **If listing multiple targets, prioritize active markets** (e.g. London, then Germany, then the Netherlands, then the rest of the EU). The US pays more but visas (e.g. H-1B) are a lottery many companies won't play.
- **Low-tax jurisdictions can be an automatic barrier** — companies hiring from them face extra tax and legal load, which makes hiring harder.
- **"Remote" is often geographically bounded** — frequently US-only or Europe-only for tax and labor reasons, and you may not learn the boundary until you're rejected. LinkedIn is weak for remote search; niche channels that post remote roles work better. Companies with a legal entity or an employer-of-record (e.g. Deel) in your location can hire you there.

### Describing achievements

Achievements should be concrete, with numbers, tied to a metric — not a money amount.

| ❌ Vague | ✅ Concrete — action + metric + how measured |
|---|---|
| "Wrote code and shipped features" | "Shipped a feature that raised sales ~10% via UX, measured in analytics" |
| "Increased sales" | "Increased year-over-year revenue ~5%" |
| "Improved engagement" | "Raised DAU ~30%, measured via the observability stack" |
| Exact figure under NDA | "Grew users by tens of thousands" — a range, no trade secret |

- If you lack exact data, use orders of magnitude or percentages ("increased the metric by tens of percent").
- For senior/manager work, add scale and timeline: "Launched from scratch, hired a team of 5, grew to 20 in six months, built delivery processes, which moved metric X by Y%."

**NDA:** confidentiality typically restricts disclosure for a few years after leaving. Speak in ranges or percentages ("grew users by tens of thousands") rather than exact figures. Not everything is covered — a result that reveals no trade secret can be mentioned. Tie numbers to metrics, not to money; talking openly about money can read as a confidentiality breach. Money figures also invite scrutiny — a non-managerial employee rarely has access to revenue data, so quoting it raises questions rather than settling them. Anchor numbers to product metrics you'd plausibly own.

**Team achievements:** if a result was the team's, you can list it, but be ready to explain your specific contribution in the interview. Not being able to measure your impact is itself a career problem — knowing how your work affects the business, even indirectly, is an advantage. Tracking achievements year over year is rare but useful; keep a running note so you have data for interviews.

Industry is rarely a red flag on its own — the barrier is usually location and its tax implications, not the sector. Some sectors are even a plus: adult and gaming companies (Pornhub, for example) run serious engineering for content delivery, retention, and scale, and that experience is valued when recruiters understand the domain.

**Referrals** are the primary channel. Many companies run referral programs that pay employees a bonus (commonly ~$1,000–1,500 for a mid-level hire retained past six months), so employees are motivated to refer. Some companies route referrals through intermediary staffing firms to cut hiring costs — the more people employed through such a firm, the lower the company's overhead.

- Find them in communities, meetups, and conferences, where it's easy to ask directly: "I saw you're hiring — can you refer me?"
- Make it easy to say yes: have a resume, a short pitch on why you fit, and ideally the job link ready. A referral system usually takes the resume plus the referrer's comment tied to a posting.
- **Better still, ask to be introduced to the hiring manager** (works from senior level up). Find them on LinkedIn, ask who owns the role, request a short call — coffee or Zoom — to discuss the role and fit. It's a pre-introduction, not an interview. Managers want to grow their network and remember interested candidates for future openings.

In-person beats a digital footprint: after meeting you, people refer with more confidence — and you also learn who wouldn't work with you, which saves time. Informal gatherings count; showing up when the local devs go for a beer is worth it.

**Cold outreach** when meetups aren't available (e.g. targeting another country): message people on the team from the posting, especially developers who can refer. "Hi, I saw this opening [link], I'd like to apply — could you refer me? Resume attached." Roughly 10% reply; the rest ignore, which is normal. Conversion is higher at larger companies where referrals feel routine. A clear, specific message stands out against the recruiter spam engineers already ignore. LinkedIn is built for this and cold messages are the norm there; Twitter is harder, Facebook harder still. Cold-messaging recruiters directly has little upside — they earn no referral bonus and will only route you through the standard process (occasionally a proactive one shepherds a candidate, but that's the exception).

The flip side of high volume is fraud. Interview proxying — where one person sits the technical interview for another — is a known pattern, and it's part of the noise hiring managers screen for. It rarely affects you directly, but it's why some processes feel needlessly suspicious.

**Recruiters and headhunters:**

- Independent agency recruiters handle less prestigious roles but are a good source for interview practice and company introductions. Good agency recruiters know the stacks (e.g. that TypeScript candidates can apply to JavaScript roles). A recruiter contact often means a technical interview or a light screen next.
- Headhunters source narrow specialists against specific requirements, earn large fees, and only forward candidates they rate — so being put forward means high odds, short of failing the interview.
- Get to know independent recruiters, stay on their radar; they're a steady source of live contacts.

| | Agency / independent recruiter | Headhunter |
|---|---|---|
| Sources for | Any fitting candidate, broad roles | Narrow specialists vs strict criteria |
| Fees | Standard | Large |
| Best for you | Interview practice, company intros | High odds — they only forward who they rate |

Work the relationship: recruiters' KPIs are often tied to a hire staying six months to two years, so they want you to pass. After a screen, ask for feedback and what to prepare next. After an interview, follow up with your strengths ("showed experience with high-performance React components") — at high candidate volume, this reminds them what to relay to the manager.

### English level

- **Mid:** B1 is enough if you can explain yourself (also enough for a Blue Card in the Netherlands or Germany).
- **Senior:** B1 with limited experience; B2 preferred with more, since you need to express detail.
- Better language means less room for discrimination. If you read technical material without a dictionary and people understand you, that's enough — a certificate usually isn't required.
- If a posting asks for higher, apply anyway; strong experience earns leeway. For roles not centered on communication, level matters less.

### A hard-to-pronounce name

A name recruiters stumble over is a friction point and, in practice, a discrimination vector. If yours is hard to pronounce, offer a short form (Aleksey → Alex, Vladislav → Vlad) — it lowers the barrier on calls and in writing. Surnames matter less; people rarely address you by them. Ask a native speaker whether your name reads easily; if interviewers hesitate on it, a simpler handle helps.

### Reading demand

Ignore the general "market state." People find work in downturns. What actually drives demand:

- **Company budgets.** Cuts to engineering spend lower demand; you rarely see this directly.
- **How the company makes money.** Software companies value engineers more and ride their own cycles. In 2022, B2C grew while B2B SaaS dipped. Service businesses (outsourcing, consulting) follow seasonal and client-driven demand. Companies where software isn't the core (banks, insurers) run their own market, often prefer cheaper labor, and become easier to enter after big-tech layoffs — they favor candidates with similar-sector experience.
- **Company stage.** Early stage wants top specialists; growth stage hires aggressively; steady profit wants hands and hires slowly; new directions need whole teams. During contractions the market floods with candidates.
- **Company location.** A global company hiring in the UK follows UK hiring rules and local pay; a US company's London entity does not pay US salaries. Companies open local entities to access a local market.

Companies prefer candidates from similar sectors: to move into product, start at any product company; the same holds for outsourcing or banking.

Two market quirks worth knowing. Some companies hire in bulk mainly to keep talent from competitors — Microsoft has done this to deny hires to Google — which makes it easier to get in during that phase, though it's hard to spot one from outside. And booms reverse: in the 2021 US surge, changing jobs alone brought ~1.5x pay, and many of those hires were laid off soon after.

### Local experience and relocation

Without local experience you're an unknown, which reads as risk — and to employers that risk is concrete. Berlin recruiters reported around 2018 that roughly 15% of Eastern European candidates who signed a contract never showed up for the job — one in six — so everyone gets painted with the same brush. In Berlin, offers can run ~20% lower for someone with no local track record than for someone who simply showed up to a meetup and met people.

A practical relocation path: take any role on the target market — even a startup at lower pay — to get the visa and residence (Blue Card, PR). After a year, switch as a local candidate with local experience. In Berlin, local experience can add ~10–15k to a senior offer.

State relocation needs early. A visa or relocation requirement is a dealbreaker for many companies, especially in the US. Some hire remotely worldwide but expect relocation to the US in 2–3 years; if that's not discussed up front, the parting is unpleasant for both sides.

### Mid vs senior, and planning

There are far more mid-level openings than senior ones, but seniors have an easier time: fewer interviews, less competition, higher odds per application. When choosing between applying as mid or senior, aim higher.

Planning a career:

- **Start from your own experience.** If a theme you enjoy exists (e.g. three years optimizing Go backend services), pick a segment — banks, insurers, product companies — and build depth there. If there's no theme yet, take varied work to build range — developing taste in a career is like in art: you have to see many examples before you know what you like.
- **Plan two steps ahead**, not one. Optimizing only for the next salary risks a local maximum with no deep expertise, which stalls when the market shifts. If the end goal (e.g. big-tech) is out of reach now, find an intermediate product company for 2–3 years. This also gives a real answer to "where do you see yourself in five years." On interviews, probe the growth path directly ("Could I become the Go expert here?") and read the interviewers' reaction — it tells you whether that future is realistic.

**Specialization vs generalization:** over 5–10 years, specialization grows career and pay faster. Long term, generalists transfer better across contexts — seven years of only one language makes switching hard. Broaden responsibility over time.

| Horizon | Winner | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Short (5–10 years) | Specialist | Faster career and pay growth; easier to get hired |
| Long term | Generalist | Skills transfer across contexts; less lock-in when tech ages out |

At senior+ a fork appears: technical depth (DB tuning, sharding, large data) or business problems (cloud choice, analytics). They're hard to combine — you can't credibly position as an expert in both. Technical specialists are sold through conferences and reputation; business solvers through consultants, headhunters, and business events. Avoid betting the whole brand on a single framework that can age out; bet on engineering or business depth instead.

To stand out past confident-senior, build on a genuine strength rather than the median "writes features and tests" path — it lowers competition. A strong mentor who actively promotes you matters more the higher you aim.

Concrete trajectories vary widely. An engineer who built a widely used product (e.g. Mike Perham with Sidekiq) can move into entrepreneurship or consulting, selling an outcome rather than engineering hours. A deep-technical path toward someone like Jeff Dean depends on luck, sponsors, soft skills, and a mentor who promotes you — hard to plan directly. Technical specialists on narrow, measurable problems (platform optimization) can earn a lot precisely because their impact is easy to quantify; business solvers earn well too but are harder to sell. A concrete niche like payment systems is attractive because it pairs a clear business domain with an engineering core, so it sells easily.
